Standard Error for Sample Proportions

  • For random samples of size \(n\) from a population with proportion \(p\), the standard error of the sample proportion \(\hat p\) is

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \]

  • The larger the sample size \(n\), the smaller the SE.

Have them check the formula against the StatKey values they just recorded: 0.0335 for n = 100 and 0.0150 for n = 500.

The Central Limit Theorem for Proportions

  • If \(n\) is sufficiently large, \[ \hat p \sim N\left(p,\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\right) \]

  • A normal distribution is a good approximation as long as

    • \(np\ge 10\) and

    • \(n(1-p)\ge 10\)

Confidence Interval for a Single Proportion

A confidence interval for a population proportion \(p\) can be computed using \[ \hat p \pm z^* \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} \]

if \(n\) is large enough so that

  • \(n\hat p\ge 10\) and

  • \(n (1-\hat p)\ge 10\)

Example 2: Movie Goers

In a random sample of 1000 US adults in August 2022, 550 of them said they are more likely to wait and watch a new movie in the comfort of their own home.

  1. Check the sample size conditions and find a 95% confidence interval for the proportion of movie goers who are more likely to watch a new movie from home. (Use \(z^* = 1.96\).)
  1. Interpret the interval in context.

\(\hat p = 0.55\); conditions: 550 and 450 are both at least 10. SE = sqrt(0.55 x 0.45 / 1000) = 0.0157. ME = 1.96 x 0.0157 = 0.0308. 95% CI: 0.55 plus or minus 0.031, i.e. about (0.519, 0.581).

Interpretation: we are 95% confident that between about 51.9% and 58.1% of movie goers (US adults) are more likely to wait and watch a new movie at home.

Hypothesis Testing for a Single Proportion

To test \(H_0:p=p_0\), we use the standardized test statistic \[ z=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}} \] where \(\hat p\) is the proportion in a random sample of size \(n\).

  • Check sample size conditions:
    • \(np_0\ge 10\)
    • \(n(1-p_0)\ge 10\)

Computing the P-value

  • The appropriate \(p\)-value depends on the alternative hypothesis:
Alternate Hypothesis \(p\)-value
\(H_a: p>p_0\) \(P(Z\ge z)\)
\(H_a: p < p_0\) \(P(Z\le z)\)
\(H_a:p\neq p_0\) \(2P(Z\ge z)\) if \(z\ge 0\)
\(H_a:p\neq p_0\) \(2P(Z\le z)\) if \(z<0\)

Tongue Rolling: The Test

Tongue rolling has been said to be a dominant trait, in which case theoretically 75% of all people should be able to roll their tongues. Do our data provide evidence otherwise?

  1. State the null and alternative hypotheses.
  1. Check the sample size conditions.

  1. \(H_0: p = 0.75\) versus \(H_a: p \ne 0.75\) (two-sided because the question asks for evidence “otherwise”).

  2. Need \(np_0 = 0.75n \ge 10\) (n at least 14) and \(n(1-p_0) = 0.25n \ge 10\) (n at least 40). Heads up: if the class has fewer than 40 students, the second condition fails. Options: pool with the other section or last term’s survey data, or acknowledge the normal approximation is shaky and use StatKey’s randomization test for a single proportion.

Tongue Test, Continued

Recall: the claim is that 75% of all people can roll their tongues.

  1. Find the test statistic and the \(p\)-value.
  1. State your conclusion in context.

  1. \(z = (\hat p - 0.75)/\sqrt{0.75(0.25)/n}\); p-value is \(2P(Z \ge |z|)\).

  2. Compare the p-value to a significance level (0.05): small p-value means the data provide evidence the proportion differs from 0.75; large p-value means no evidence against 75% (not proof that it is 75%). Also remind them of the sampling caveat.

Dice: Testing for Fairness

Let \(p\) be the proportion of rolls (ignoring ties) in which the white die is greater than the red die.

  1. If both dice are fair, what value should \(p\) have, and why?
  1. State the null and alternative hypotheses for testing whether the dice are fair.

  1. For two fair dice, P(white greater) = 15/36, P(red greater) = 15/36, P(tie) = 6/36. Ignoring ties leaves 15/30 = 0.5, so \(p = 0.5\).

  2. \(H_0: p = 0.5\) versus \(H_a: p \ne 0.5\).

Do not reveal that the dice are rigged yet.

Sample Size for a Desired Margin of Error

To estimate a population proportion to within a desired margin of error, \(\text{ME}\) with a given level of confidence, choose \(n\) such that \[ n=\left(\frac{z^*}{\text{ME}}\right)^2 \tilde p (1-\tilde p) \]

where \(\tilde p\) is either

  • A guessed value for \(p\) (if one is available) OR

  • \(\tilde p=0.5\) if no other guess is available

    • Setting \(\tilde p=0.5\) will ensure that \(n\) is large enough no matter what \(p\) turns out to be.

Natural stopping point: if time is short, start next class here, with Example 3 and then move on to the next section.

Example 3: Sample Size

Recall (Example 2): \(n = 1000\), \(\hat p = 0.55\), 95% confidence (\(z^* = 1.96\)).

  1. What is the margin of error for the confidence interval found in Example 2?
  1. What sample size is needed if we want a margin of error within ±2%? (Use the sample proportion from the original sample.)
  1. What sample size is needed if we want a margin of error within ±2%, and if we use the conservative estimate of \(p = 0.5\)?

  1. ME = 1.96 x sqrt(0.55 x 0.45 / 1000) = 0.0308 (about 3.1 percentage points); it is the “plus or minus” part of the interval.

  2. \(n = (1.96/0.02)^2(0.55)(0.45) = 2376.99\), so n = 2377 (always round up).

  3. \(n = (1.96/0.02)^2(0.5)(0.5) = 2401\).

Compare (b) and (c): the conservative estimate asks for slightly more people but guarantees the ME no matter what p turns out to be.

Wrapping Up

  • The SE of \(\hat p\) shrinks as \(n\) grows, and \(\hat p\) is approximately normal when \(np \ge 10\) and \(n(1-p) \ge 10\).
  • A confidence interval for \(p\) uses \(\hat p\) in the SE: \(\hat p \pm z^* \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\).
  • A test of \(H_0: p = p_0\) uses \(p_0\) in the SE, and the conditions are checked with \(p_0\).
  • The same data can be used to estimate \(p\) (interval) or to check a claim about \(p\) (test).
  • Plan sample size from the margin of error you want, using \(\tilde p = 0.5\) when you have no guess.
